An expired mortgage pre-approval is one of those moments in the homebuying journey that feels more alarming than it actually is. You've done the hard work, a lender already reviewed your finances, and now a date on a piece of paper says you're back to square one. You're not. Renewal is usually free, faster than the original process, and—if your financial picture hasn't changed—largely administrative. What matters is moving quickly and methodically, so here is exactly how to do that.

## What "Expired" Actually Means—and What It Doesn't

A pre-approval letter is a lender's conditional commitment to lend you up to a specific amount based on a verified snapshot of your finances at a point in time. 
Most lenders issue letters valid for 60, 90, or 120 days from the issue date, with 90 days being the most common standard because it aligns with the 120-day credit-report shelf life and leaves room for offer, contract, and closing inside the same verification cycle.
 When that window closes, the snapshot is simply considered stale—the lender can no longer stand behind the numbers without a refresh.

What expiration does not mean: your credit is ruined, your relationship with the lender is over, or your purchase is dead. 
There is no rule preventing a buyer from submitting an offer with an expired letter attached, though most listing agents and sellers will reject it or ask for a current letter before considering the offer seriously.
 That makes renewal a practical necessity rather than a bureaucratic formality. The good news is that 
if your pre-approval expires before closing, you'll need to renew it—usually free—or reapply.

## Step 1: Contact Your Lender the Same Day You Realize It's Lapsed

The single most important action is also the simplest: pick up the phone or send a message to your loan officer today. Do not wait until you have found a house or are ready to make an offer. Lenders already have most of your original file on record, which means the renewal conversation starts from a much more favorable position than a cold application. Ask two specific questions: whether they treat the renewal as a file refresh or a brand-new application, and whether there will be any fee involved.

This call also lets your lender flag any potential issues before you're standing in front of a seller trying to make an offer. If your financial situation has changed—a new employer, a car loan you took out during your search, a credit card balance that crept up—your loan officer needs to know now, not on the day you need the letter.

## Step 2: Gather Your Updated Documents

When your pre-approval expires, you'll need to provide updated financial documents like recent pay stubs and bank statements, and your mortgage lender may perform another hard credit pull.
 The keyword is "updated"—lenders don't want last quarter's documents; they want to see your current financial reality. Here is what to have ready:

- **Pay stubs:** Typically the most recent 30 days. If you're paid biweekly, that's usually your last two stubs.
- **Bank statements:** Most lenders want the last two to three months for all accounts you'll use toward the down payment and reserves.
- **W-2s or tax returns:** If it has been long enough that a new tax year has closed since your original application, expect to provide the most recent year's returns. 
You'll generally need recent pay stubs, tax returns covering two years, bank statements covering two to three months, employment verification, and identification.

- **Employment verification:** If you've changed jobs since the original letter, your lender will need to verify your new employer, start date, and compensation structure.
- **Asset documentation:** Any accounts showing your down payment funds, especially if the balance has changed materially.

The faster you assemble these documents, the faster your renewal moves. 
If your documentation is complete and consistent, pre-approval is often issued within 24 to 72 hours. If questions arise—such as income fluctuations or unexplained deposits—the lender may request clarification, which extends the timeline.
 Straightforward W-2 borrowers who upload clean documents often get a refreshed letter the same day or the next business day.

## Step 3: Understand the Credit Pull—and Why It Shouldn't Scare You

The question buyers ask most often about renewal is whether the process will hurt their credit score. The answer depends on how the lender handles the file refresh. 
It depends on how the lender pulls credit at renewal. A soft pull, which many lenders use for file refreshes, has no effect on your score. A hard pull, which is required if the renewed letter is being issued as a current commitment, can drop your score by 2 to 5 points temporarily.

Even in the hard-pull scenario, the real-world impact is modest. 
In general, credit inquiries have a small impact on your FICO scores. For most people, one additional credit inquiry will take less than five points off their FICO scores.
 And if you're also shopping rates with other lenders at the same time—which is a smart move—the rate-shopping rules work in your favor. 
This is called a "rate shopping window," and it lasts 45 days for the newer versions of the FICO scores. For FICO scores calculated from older versions of the scoring formula, this shopping period is any 14-day span.
 To be conservative and cover both older and newer scoring models, 
limiting your loan applications to a short window can reduce the negative impact to your credit score—doing all your rate shopping within 14 days limits the impact no matter which scoring models your lenders use.

The bigger credit risk during a renewal window is what you do between letters, not the pull itself: opening a new credit card or financing a car can move your score more than the renewal inquiry will.
 Keep your credit behavior steady from the moment your original letter was issued through closing, and the renewal pull will be a non-event.

## Step 4: Treat the Renewal as a Financial Checkpoint

If your situation has not changed materially, renewal is little more than an administrative update. But if anything significant has shifted—a job change, a new debt obligation, a credit score that moved—this is the moment to understand exactly where you stand, not after you've already made an offer.

A change in employment is one of the most common triggers for a more involved review. Lenders generally want to see stability, and a new job—even at higher pay—can raise questions about probationary periods and income continuity. A higher debt load directly affects your debt-to-income (DTI) ratio, which is one of the primary factors lenders use to set your approved loan amount. If your DTI has increased enough, you may qualify for less house than your original letter indicated. On the flip side, circumstances can move in your favor too. 
A fresh pre-approval can unlock a higher purchase price, a better rate tier, or lower mortgage insurance than the original letter offered. The renewal conversation is also the right time to revisit those numbers and decide whether locking your rate early in the next offer cycle makes sense for your situation.

Use this checkpoint deliberately. Pull your own credit report before calling your lender so you arrive with a clear picture of what they'll see. Review your outstanding debt balances, and if there are any errors on your report, start the dispute process immediately—even a few weeks of lead time can matter.

## Step 5: Set a Calendar System That Prevents Future Expirations

The easiest way to avoid this situation again is to treat your pre-approval letter the same way you treat a passport or a driver's license: know the expiration date and act before it arrives. 
The exact expiration date should be printed on the letter, usually in the footer or fine print. If the date is not listed, call your loan officer to confirm the lender's policy.

Once you have that date, set a calendar reminder two weeks out with a simple note: "Contact lender about pre-approval renewal." Two weeks gives you enough runway to gather updated documents and get a refreshed letter without any pressure. If you're in active offer negotiations and worried a closing delay might push you past the letter date, alert your lender early—many will issue an extension if you're already under contract.

The buyers who face the most stress around expired letters are those who discover the problem on the day they want to make an offer. A two-week buffer turns a potential crisis into a routine phone call.

## When Your Renewed Letter Might Look Different

For most buyers whose finances are stable, a renewed letter is functionally identical to the original: same lender, similar terms, updated expiration date. But there are scenarios in which the renewed letter reflects materially different terms—and being prepared for that possibility is important.

Interest rates can move between your original pre-approval and renewal. If rates have risen during your home search, your renewed letter will reflect current market conditions, which may change the monthly payment associated with your approved loan amount. This isn't a failure of the renewal process—it's the letter accurately reflecting today's lending environment. If rates have fallen, you might actually benefit from the refresh. Either way, the renewal moment is an opportunity to confirm that the lender and loan product you started with are still the right fit, rather than simply hitting resubmit and assuming the original terms are optimal.

## The Role of a Mortgage Advisor at Renewal Time

Renewing on your own is entirely doable, particularly if your financial situation is unchanged and your existing lender is responsive. The renewal steps above are straightforward for the average W-2 borrower with a clean file. That said, the renewal moment is one of the best natural opportunities in the homebuying timeline to get a second opinion on your terms.

A mortgage advisor—whether that's your existing loan officer or one you bring in for a comparison—can run a quick review of your current financial profile against the rate environment that exists today, not the one that existed when you first applied. Markets shift over the course of a 90-day search. A rate that was competitive in the spring may not be the best available in the summer. Bringing a second lender into the picture during your renewal window costs you nothing in credit-score terms if you make both inquiries within the rate-shopping window, and it ensures that the letter you renew isn't simply the easiest one to get—it's the best one available to you. That's a distinction worth a single extra phone call.
