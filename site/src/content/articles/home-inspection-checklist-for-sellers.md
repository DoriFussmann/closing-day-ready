# Home Inspection Checklist for Sellers: What to Fix First

Most sellers spend weeks staging, pricing, and marketing their home — and then treat the home inspection as something that happens *to* them rather than something they can actively shape. That's a costly miscalculation. 
The inspection period is when more deals fall apart than at any other stage of the transaction.
 Understanding what an inspector will flag, addressing the easy issues in advance, and knowing which larger problems to fix versus disclose versus credit can be the difference between a clean closing and a renegotiated deal that costs you thousands.

This checklist is built around the decisions sellers actually face — not a generic walkthrough of what inspectors do, but a practical, room-by-room framework for getting ahead of the report before it lands in your buyer's inbox.

---

## Why Sellers Who Prep Win at the Negotiating Table

Research analyzing thousands of home inspections finds that 86% uncover issues requiring attention, with buyers negotiating an average of $14,000 off the sale price using inspection findings. The most common problems include roof issues at 19.7% of inspections, electrical problems at 18.7%, and window defects at 18.4%.
 Those aren't obscure structural failures — many are entirely preventable findings that a prepared seller could have resolved with an afternoon and a hardware store run.

A buyer's inspector spends two to four hours examining every accessible system in your home, and the resulting report can contain dozens of findings that give buyers ammunition to renegotiate, request costly repairs, or walk away entirely.
 When the report is long, buyers often don't distinguish between a $12 outlet cover and a $12,000 roof repair — they see a number of findings and feel anxiety. Your job as a seller is to reduce that number, especially the line items that are cheap to fix but look alarming on paper. The sellers who come out ahead treat the inspection as something they actively prepare for and, in a real sense, manage
 — not a passive event they simply endure.

## The Low-Cost Fixes That Make the Biggest Difference

Before hiring anyone or spending significant money, do a complete self-walkthrough of your home with fresh eyes — ideally with someone who hasn't lived there and can see things you've stopped noticing.

### Electrical and Safety Items

Missing outlet covers, loose switch plates, and non-functioning outlets are among the most common line items in inspection reports, and each one costs under a dollar to fix. 
A GFCI outlet is a safety device that protects against electrical shock by monitoring electrical current and quickly shutting off power if an imbalance occurs. GFCI protection is required in one of two forms — either at the outlet itself or as a GFCI breaker in the electrical panel — and is required anytime an outlet is within six feet of water, outside, or in a garage.
 Walk every bathroom, kitchen, garage, and exterior outlet and confirm GFCI protection is in place. A licensed electrician can add a GFCI outlet for roughly $100–$200 per location — far cheaper than what a buyer will ask for in a credit.

Also check every light fixture in the home. Burned-out bulbs seem trivial, but inspectors note them because they can't verify whether a fixture is functioning or whether there's a wiring issue behind the dead bulb. Replace them all, including in closets, basements, and attics.

Homes with older electrical panels — over 20 years — are inspected twice as often for safety issues.
 If your panel is aging, make sure the area around it is clear and accessible. Inspectors flag blocked panels, and an inspector who can't fully examine a panel will note it as a limitation — which reads worse than it sounds.

### Plumbing and Water

Forty-five percent of inspections find problems related to water intrusion and drainage.
 Water is the single issue buyers fear most because it suggests hidden damage, mold risk, and ongoing repair cycles. Before inspection day, check under every sink for drips or water stains, test all toilets for running (the flapper is a $10 part), confirm that shower and tub caulk is intact and mold-free, and verify that all drains flow freely. Look at the water heater: inspectors check for corrosion, proper pressure-relief valve installation, and strapping in seismic zones.

Check exterior hose bibs for drips and confirm they shut off completely. These are inexpensive fixes individually, but a string of them in a report signals a home that hasn't been maintained.

### HVAC, Roof, and Attic — The Big Three

These are the categories where buyers feel the most financial risk, and where a seller's pre-inspection effort pays the highest return. 
It's crucial to maintain appliances and inspect plumbing and HVAC systems as part of any serious pre-inspection preparation.

**HVAC:** Replace the air filter — this costs under $20 and signals regular maintenance instantly. If you haven't had the system professionally serviced in the past 12 months, schedule a tune-up before the inspection. A service receipt dated within the past year sitting in your documentation packet communicates far more than a clean filter alone. Clear at least two to three feet of space around the air handler and outdoor condenser unit; blocked equipment gets noted.

**Roof:** You don't need to replace your roof before listing, but you should know its condition. Walk the perimeter and look for visible damage — missing shingles, sagging sections, or lifted flashing. Clean the gutters completely; clogged gutters are a nearly universal inspection flag because they suggest poor drainage management and can cause fascia rot, water intrusion, and foundation issues. If gutters are pulling away from the roofline, re-secure them.

**Attic:** Inspectors look for signs of active leaks, inadequate ventilation, and missing or disturbed insulation. Make sure the attic access hatch is easily reachable and that nothing is stored directly blocking it.

### Interior Room-by-Room

Work through each room methodically:

- **Doors and windows:** Every door and window should open, close, and latch properly. Sticking doors or windows can indicate foundation movement or moisture swelling — even when neither is the cause, they raise flags. Lubricate hinges, adjust strike plates, and confirm window locks engage.
- **Smoke and carbon monoxide detectors:** 
Approximately 25% of homes have outdated or faulty smoke detectors.
 Test every detector, replace batteries, and check manufacture dates — most have a ten-year lifespan. CO detectors are required on every level with sleeping areas in most jurisdictions.
- **Walls and ceilings:** Fill and paint small nail holes and scuff marks. More importantly, look for any evidence of prior water damage — staining, bubbling paint, or soft drywall — and address the source before the inspection, not just the cosmetic result.
- **Basement and crawl space:** Ensure access is unobstructed. Remove storage that blocks the inspector's view of the foundation walls. Any visible efflorescence (white mineral deposits) on foundation walls should be cleaned; it's often a sign of past moisture that you may want to be prepared to discuss.

### Exterior and Curb Appeal

First impressions matter to buyers, but they also matter to inspectors. A home that looks well-maintained on the outside signals that the inside has been cared for too. Clean gutters, trim vegetation away from the home's siding and foundation (plants in contact with siding are an entry point for moisture and pests), and confirm that the grade around the foundation slopes away from the house rather than toward it. Make sure all exterior lighting functions and that deck or porch boards are secure and not rotting.

## The Fix vs. Disclose vs. Credit Framework

Not every defect warrants a repair. Sellers who try to fix everything before listing often over-invest in items buyers would have preferred to handle themselves — or receive as a closing credit they can apply toward something they actually want. The right question for each defect is: *Should I fix it, disclose it, or offer a credit?*

**Fix it** when the item is:
- A safety issue (non-functioning smoke detectors, missing GFCI protection, exposed wiring)
- Something that could affect financing approval (FHA and VA loans, for example, have stricter property condition requirements that can delay or kill financing)
- Inexpensive enough that doing it yourself costs less than the negotiating friction it will create

**Disclose it** when the item is:
- A known condition you cannot fully remediate (e.g., a prior repair to a roof leak, even if the leak is resolved)
- Something an inspector will certainly find anyway — proactive disclosure signals honesty and reduces buyer anxiety about what else might be hidden

**Offer a credit** when the item is:
- A major system upgrade (roof replacement, HVAC replacement) that a buyer would prefer to oversee themselves
- Something where repair quality is subjective and the buyer would reasonably want their own contractor

Common seller responses to inspection findings include agreeing to specific repairs, offering a closing credit in lieu of repairs, reducing the sale price, or declining to make changes.
 Knowing which lever to pull before the report even arrives puts you in a far stronger negotiating position than reacting under deadline pressure.

## The Power of Documentation: Your Home History Packet

One of the most underutilized tools a seller has is paperwork. 
Create a professional "home history" folder and leave it on the kitchen counter. Include receipts for recent major services, list the ages of all major appliances, and provide copies of any transferable warranties or building permits for past renovations. Providing this paperwork upfront preemptively answers the inspector's questions and reinforces your position as a transparent, reliable seller.

When an inspector sees a five-year-old water heater with no visible corrosion and a receipt for last year's HVAC tune-up, the uncertainty that drives repair requests evaporates. Documentation doesn't just inform — it reassures. And reassured buyers make cleaner offers.

## Should You Order a Pre-Listing Inspection?

A pre-listing inspection — one you order and pay for before putting your home on the market — is optional, but it is increasingly being used by sellers who want maximum control over the transaction. 
Some sellers now pay for pre-listing inspections because smart sellers want to know what's wrong before buyers discover it. If they can fix problems ahead of time or price the home accordingly, they avoid renegotiations later.

The core advantage is time and choice. 
Instead of reacting to a buyer's demands under a tight deadline, you can choose which issues to fix on your own terms. If you decide not to fix something, you can disclose it upfront and price the home accordingly.
 That transparency can actually strengthen your negotiating position — buyers who feel they've received honest disclosure tend to trust the transaction more and push back less.

Nearly 60% of real estate agents recommend pre-listing inspections to sellers.
 It's a meaningful signal that the industry has come to see proactive disclosure as a sales asset, not a liability. The primary caveat: in most states, anything a pre-listing inspection uncovers becomes a required disclosure item. Going in, understand that the report may obligate you to disclose findings even if you choose not to repair them — review your state's disclosure requirements before commissioning one.

## The Coordinated Close: When You're Buying and Selling Simultaneously

Most sellers aren't just selling — they're also buying their next home at the same time. That dual transaction creates pressure that makes inspection prep even more critical. A deal that gets derailed by a long repair negotiation doesn't just delay your closing; it can jeopardize your rate lock on the mortgage you've secured for your purchase. Every day a renegotiation drags out is a day closer to losing the financing terms you locked in.

The sellers who close fastest and on the best terms are the ones who remove inspection friction proactively — the ones who fixed the running toilet, serviced the HVAC, cleared the gutters, and left a documentation packet on the counter. When the buyer's inspector produces a short report, there's less to negotiate over, and both parties get to the closing table without the escalating anxiety that long reports produce.
