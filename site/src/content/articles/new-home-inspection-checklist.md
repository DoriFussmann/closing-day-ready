## Why New Construction Is Not the Same as Defect-Free

Buying a brand-new home carries an understandable sense of security. Everything is freshly built, the builder has offered a warranty, and a city inspector has already signed off on the work. The reasonable assumption is that problems are someone else's concern now. That assumption costs buyers thousands of dollars every year.

Research has found that 65% of new construction homes have issues uncovered during inspection.
 The problems range from cosmetic to genuinely serious — 
improper HVAC installation, electrical code violations, and plumbing that wasn't pressure-tested correctly are all common findings, alongside new homes that have code violations the local inspector missed and systems not properly commissioned or tested.
 The core reason is straightforward: 
construction crews move fast, especially in busy building seasons, and multiple subcontractors work on the same house, often without seeing each other's work — and that pace creates room for mistakes.

None of this is a reason to walk away from new construction. It is a reason to inspect it properly — which means understanding that a single walkthrough at the end is not enough.

## The Three-Phase Inspection Protocol

### Phase One: Pre-Pour (Foundation) Inspection

The first independent inspection happens before concrete is poured for the foundation. At this stage, your inspector examines the excavation, soil preparation, placement of rebar and wire mesh, forms, and any underground plumbing that will be encased in the slab. 
Even in new construction, inspectors often find problems such as foundation cracks, uneven settling, or improper framing — issues that can arise from inadequate site preparation, poor-quality concrete, or shortcuts taken during construction.
 Once the concrete is poured, none of these components are accessible again without breaking through the slab — an expensive and disruptive process.

Not every builder's timeline will give you an obvious window for this inspection, which is why it is worth discussing the construction schedule early and asking your builder explicitly for 48–72 hours' notice before the pour. Put that request in writing.

### Phase Two: Pre-Drywall (MEP) Inspection

The pre-drywall inspection — also called the mechanical, electrical, and plumbing inspection — is widely considered the most critical of the three phases. 
In new builds, this inspection happens after framing, wiring, plumbing, and HVAC rough-in are complete but before the drywall goes up.
 It is your one and only opportunity to see inside the walls.

Missing or improperly installed flashing, and a lack of attic ventilation all appear prevalently on inspection reports
 at this stage. Electrical concerns are equally common: 
faulty wiring is a common defect in new homes, and wires that are not properly connected or that may be overloaded due to poor planning during construction are frequently documented.
 On the HVAC side, inspectors look at whether ductwork is sealed, whether equipment is sized appropriately for the home's actual layout, and whether the system has been commissioned — meaning tested to confirm it actually performs as designed. 
Twenty to thirty percent air loss is typical in homes with unsealed ductwork, and a new, energy-efficient home with high-performance air sealing can create an unexpected secondary problem — poor indoor air quality — if the home lacks a balanced ventilation system.

Skipping the pre-drywall inspection is not a minor oversight. Any defect in framing, wiring, plumbing, or HVAC rough-in that goes undetected here will be covered by drywall, flooring, and finishes — and uncovering it later means tearing those finishes back out.

### Phase Three: Final Walkthrough Inspection

The final inspection takes place when the home is complete and ready for closing. This is the phase most buyers already know about, but many approach it passively — walking through with the builder's representative and nodding along. Treat it instead as a structured audit with a written punch list in hand.

Missing insulation, improperly sloped grading, HVAC systems installed incorrectly, and electrical panels with loose connections are common findings
 at the final stage. Additional items that routinely appear on final-phase reports include:

- **Grading and drainage** — soil that slopes toward the foundation rather than away from it. 
Water damage can wreak havoc on a home, and in new constructions, inadequate drainage or improper grading around the property can result in water pooling near the foundation or infiltrating the basement or crawlspace.

- **Cabinet and finish quality** — unlevel cabinets, gaps in caulking in wet areas, misaligned doors and windows. 
Windows and doors that are out of square, inadequately flashed, or uncaulked can cause drafts, water intrusion, or pest infestations — details that have a direct impact on comfort, energy efficiency, and long-term maintenance costs.

- **Cosmetic defects signaling deeper issues** — 
cosmetic issues like uneven paint or poorly installed flooring may seem minor, but they often indicate rushed or substandard work, and inspectors frequently find misaligned trim, bubbling paint, or gaps in flooring that can signal deeper construction flaws.

## The Code Inspector Is Not Your Inspector

One of the most important things to understand about new construction is the distinction between a municipal code inspector and an independent inspector you hire. 
A municipal code inspector's job is to confirm that construction meets minimum local standards, not to advocate for you as a buyer.
 They check boxes; they do not write reports you can use in a negotiation or a warranty claim. 
Experienced home inspectors who are familiar with new construction will catch issues that builders and municipal reviews are likely to miss.

Code compliance and build quality are not synonymous. A home can pass every required inspection and still have HVAC ductwork that leaks a quarter of its conditioned air before it reaches a room, cabinets installed out of level, and flashing that will allow water intrusion within two rainy seasons. These are quality failures, not code violations — and they are the inspector's domain, not the municipality's.

## The Punch List: Your Most Valuable Document Before Signing

The punch list is the written record of every item the builder needs to correct before you accept the home. It is not a wish list or a negotiating tactic — it is a formal document that defines the condition you are agreeing to accept when you sign the final acceptance paperwork.

The sequence matters enormously. Your punch list should be compiled, submitted to the builder, and ideally resolved — or at minimum acknowledged in writing — **before** you sign any document described as a "Final Acceptance" or "Certificate of Completion." Once you have signed that document, your leverage shifts sharply. Items that were outstanding before signing become far harder to enforce, because the builder can argue that you accepted the home in its current condition.

Walk through your punch list with your independent inspector present, not just with the builder's representative. Photograph every item. Number every item. Submit it in writing and request a written response with a correction timeline. If items remain uncorrected at closing, ensure they are documented in your closing paperwork as outstanding obligations — not oral promises.

## Builder Warranties: Knowing Which Tier Applies Changes Everything

Most new construction homes come with a tiered builder warranty: a 1-year workmanship warranty covering defects in materials and workmanship, a 2-year systems warranty covering electrical, plumbing, and HVAC systems, and a 10-year structural warranty covering major structural defects.
 Understanding these tiers is not just useful background knowledge — it determines how urgently a defect needs to be documented and reported.

A crack in drywall finishing is a workmanship issue. Report it in month one, and it is covered. Wait until month fourteen, and it is your expense. An HVAC system that was never properly balanced is a systems issue — you have two years, but the clock starts at closing. Foundation settlement that affects the structural integrity of the home falls under the 10-year tier, but "structural" is typically defined narrowly, and builders will often dispute whether a given defect qualifies.

Many defects do not reveal themselves until the home has gone through a full seasonal cycle — by month 11, you still have time to act, but only if you know what to look for.
 This is exactly why a separate inspection near the end of your first year of ownership is worth scheduling. 
Industry data shows that 80–90% of new homes reveal at least one warrantable defect at the 11-month mark — issues that builders are obligated to fix at no cost to you. Spending a few hundred dollars on a detailed inspection can save $1,000 or more in costly repairs by getting the builder to fix defects covered under warranty instead of paying out of pocket later.

## A Note on Timing, Financing, and Your Builder's Closing Date

New construction purchases move on the builder's schedule, not yours. A builder may push your closing date earlier when a phase of construction finishes ahead of schedule, or delay it when supply chains or labor shortages push things back. Either scenario can create pressure on your financing.

Pre-approval letters expire — typically in 60 to 90 days — and if your builder's closing date slips past your approval window, you may need to requalify at whatever interest rate environment exists at that moment. Similarly, if the builder accelerates the timeline, you need your financing in order immediately, with no scrambling required. The inspection phase is part of this timeline: if you need to push back closing to complete a proper punch-list review, you need the flexibility in your financing to do so without penalty.

That is why staying current on your mortgage guidance matters as much as staying current on your inspection checklist. Before your builder's closing date arrives — and especially if that date moves — verify that your pre-approval is still valid, your rate lock window still covers you, and your lender has what they need. ## Where Things Stand
<!-- WHERE-THINGS-STAND:START -->
The new construction inspection landscape is evolving notably in mid-2026. 
The Congressional Budget Office projects new home construction to reach an annual average of 1.68 million units between 2025 and 2029 — compared to an average of 1.3 million units the previous decade — and industry observers expect that volume to drive meaningfully higher demand for new construction inspections.
 Against that backdrop, 
11-month warranty inspections are emerging as a standard service in high-growth markets, occurring just before the builder's one-year warranty expires so homeowners can formally document defects before their recourse period ends.

Buyers and lenders are increasingly demanding more detailed due diligence beyond the standard inspection, with bundled inspection protocols — covering foundation, HVAC, roof, and crawl space — now common, as the in-depth approach reduces the risk of major undisclosed defects that could trigger renegotiation post-inspection or post-closing.
 There are no major regulatory changes to new construction inspection requirements in the past 45 days, but the broader trend toward phased, multi-inspection protocols for new builds continues to accelerate with the housing construction boom.
